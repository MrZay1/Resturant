# Policy and Legal Research: NFC "Tap to Review on Google" Cards + Monthly AI Review Report (USA, September 2026)

Scope: what governs (1) asking restaurant guests for Google reviews with an NFC card and (2) selling a monthly AI-written analysis of a restaurant's Google reviews. All rules quoted are from primary sources where possible (Google policy pages, the CFR, FTC guidance, Stripe docs). This is research, not legal advice; the items flagged "confirm with counsel" are the ones that carry real money or account-suspension risk.

## 0. Executive summary (what actually matters for this business)

1. **Asking every guest for a Google review is allowed.** Google's own policy says merchants may "Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives," and Google's help page says "you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code." An NFC card is functionally a QR code/link. (Sources: https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114 ; https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122)
2. **Four things will get a restaurant's reviews wiped or its profile restricted:** review gating (only asking happy guests / diverting unhappy ones), any incentive (discount, free dessert, raffle), staff or family reviewing, and *pressuring guests to review while on the premises*. The last one was tightened in 2026 and is the single most important design constraint for a table-drop card: the card must be an optional invitation, never a "please do it now while I wait." (Source: https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114)
3. **As of April 17, 2026, Google also bans** "merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews" and "merchants requesting that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member." So: no per-server review quotas, no "mention me by name" scripts. Organic name mentions by guests are fine, and the AI report may read them. (Source: https://ppc.land/google-tightens-maps-review-policy-staff-names-and-quotas-now-banned/ and the policy page above)
4. **The FTC's Consumer Review Rule (16 CFR Part 465, effective Oct 21, 2024)** carries civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. It bans fake reviews, incentives conditioned on sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, and review suppression. The FTC sent its first 10 warning letters in December 2025 and obtained its first monetary settlement (TruHeight, $750,000) in July 2026. Review gating is not banned by the Rule's text, but the FTC's own FAQ says it "could violate the FTC Act," and the Endorsement Guides say flatly that sending review links only to customers likely to be happy is "misleading if it substantially skews the favorability of the reviews." (Sources: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.4 ; https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers ; https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking ; https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-warns-10-companies-about-possible-violations-agencys-new-consumer-review-rule)
5. **Shared IP / restaurant Wi-Fi is a real filter risk.** Practitioners consistently report reviews being filtered when many come from one IP (business Wi-Fi, kiosks, staff devices). Staff training must tell guests to use their own mobile data, and the restaurant should never log into its Business Profile from the guest Wi-Fi. Google does not publish its filter signals, so this is practitioner evidence, not policy text. (Source: https://whitespark.ca/blog/google-remove-reviews/)
6. **The AI report's data source must be the Google Business Profile API with the restaurant's authorization, not scraping.** The Maps Platform terms prohibit scraping; the Places API returns only 5 reviews; the Business Profile API returns all reviews but only for verified profiles where the caller is an Owner/Manager, needs an access application (verified profile 60+ days, business website), and has a 30-day cap on storing API content. This shapes the product architecture. (Sources: https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/policies ; https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/prereqs ; https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms)
7. **Naming servers in the report is legal but needs HR-safe framing and a written notice to staff.** Treat guest comments as unverified input, never as the basis for discipline or tip deductions, and watch Illinois (HB 3773, in force Jan 1, 2026) and Colorado (SB 26-189, Jan 1, 2027) AI-in-employment notice laws if any client is in those states.
8. **The website needs a Privacy Policy (CalOPPA has no size threshold; Stripe contractually requires one) and Terms of Service** covering the auto-renewing $50/month subscription (ROSCA: clear disclosure, express informed consent, simple cancellation), refunds, card replacement terms, the "no guarantee of reviews or ratings" disclaimer, and the restaurant's own compliance promises. Build to WCAG 2.1 AA; website ADA suits hit 3,117 federal filings in 2025.
9. **Trademark:** "Review us on Google" with the official Google G or wordmark is an explicitly documented use case, but: no stars next to the Google logo, no "Google-rated," no unofficial badges/lockups, no Google mark in the business name/domain/slogan, always a "Google is a trademark of Google LLC" notice, and do not use the red Maps pin (it is a registered trademark with design patents). Because the founder *sells* the printed cards, submit a brand-permission request for the card template to be safe. (Sources: https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/use-cases/customer-reviews/ ; https://about.google/brand-resource-center/guidance/ ; https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/trademarks-and-terms/terms-and-conditions/)
10. **Sales tax:** physical cards are tangible personal property, taxable in the 45 sales-tax states; the SaaS/report subscription is taxable in roughly 25-26 jurisdictions and the rules differ by state; California and Colorado start taxing electronically delivered/remote software Jan 1, 2027. Register in the home state first; use Stripe Tax or similar.

## 1. Google's review policies (Maps User Generated Content Policy + Business Profile guidance)

### 1.1 Where the rules live
- **Maps User Generated Content Policy – "Prohibited & restricted content"**: https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114 (this is the controlling document for reviews; no last-updated date is displayed on the page).
- **Glossary pages** the policy links to: "Incentivized or Biased Reviews" https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/16597558 and "Fake engagement" https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/11414422.
- **Business Profile help – "Tips to get more reviews"**: https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122.
- **Enforcement pages**: "Business Profile restrictions for policy violations" https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287 and consumer-facing "Consumer Alerts" https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/15178562.

### 1.2 The policy language, quoted

Baseline principle: "Contributions to Google Maps should reflect a genuine experience at a place or business."

**Fake Engagement** (not allowed, will be removed):
- "Content that is not based on a real experience or does not accurately represent the location"
- "Reviews or ratings that have been paid for, directly or in kind"
- "Content that has been posted from multiple accounts by or at the request of one person"
- Content posted using an "emulator or other device tampering service, modified operating system, or other method to mimic genuine engagement"

**Rating Manipulation** (not allowed):
- "Content that has been posted due to an incentive offered by a business - such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services"
- "Content exhibiting unusual volumes or patterns of review contributions that are indicative of efforts to manipulate a place's rating"
- "Content that is based on a conflict of interest," which "may include current or former employment, a contractual or consultory relationship, or other professional or personal affiliations that demonstrate a conflict of interest (such as industry competitors, familial relationships, etc.)"

Definition on the glossary page: "Incentivized reviews are reviews and ratings influenced by a payment, discount, free goods or services, or any other benefit offered to the reviewer; or biased by a personal or professional conflict of interest." and "When a business offers incentives in exchange for reviews, this undermines content integrity and can mislead consumers." (https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/16597558)

**What merchants may do**: "Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives."

**What merchants may NOT do**:
- "Offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services - in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review"
- "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews" (this is the review-gating ban)
- "Require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included. This includes:
  - Merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews
  - Merchants requesting that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member"

The "Tips to get more reviews" help page adds: "Offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews, or remove negative reviews is considered fake & misleading content and is strictly prohibited." and "To leave reviews, you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code." and "Customers must be signed into a Google Account to leave a review." (https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122)

### 1.3 Timeline of the 2026 changes (matters because most blog advice is stale)
- **Feb 20, 2026**: Search Engine Roundtable reported the "should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included" wording (secondary reporting via https://www.replyonthefly.com/blog/google-review-policy-update-2026 and https://launchcodex.com/blog/seo-geo-ai/google-business-profile-review-policy-update/).
- **Apr 16, 2026**: Google published its 2025 Trust and Safety Report (292 million policy-violating reviews blocked or removed in 2025) plus new Gemini-based Maps protections (https://launchcodex.com/blog/seo-geo-ai/google-business-profile-review-policy-update/).
- **Apr 17, 2026**: Two new Rating Manipulation clauses spotted by Google Diamond Product Expert Amy Toman: staff review quotas and staff-name/specific-content solicitation (https://ppc.land/google-tightens-maps-review-policy-staff-names-and-quotas-now-banned/).
- **Sept 2025 onward, reported June 5, 2026**: Google Maps shows in-app surveys asking users whether the business offered rewards for reviews; "yes" answers have triggered retroactive review deletions (https://ppc.land/google-maps-is-now-asking-users-if-businesses-paid-for-their-reviews/).

### 1.4 Answers to the specific questions

| Question | Answer | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Is asking every customer allowed? | Yes. Neutral, universal, incentive-free asks are the explicitly permitted activity. | "Solicit or encourage ... without offering incentives" (7400114); "ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code" (3474122) |
| Is review gating prohibited? | Yes, by Google: "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews." Routing unhappy guests to a private form *instead of* the Google link is gating. A private feedback channel offered to everyone, alongside (not instead of) the review link, is fine. | 7400114; FTC Endorsement Guides §255.2(e) Example 11 (see §2) |
| Are incentives/discounts prohibited? | Yes, any incentive "in exchange for posting any review," including for changing/removing a review. This includes raffles, free dessert, "show us your review for 10% off." | 7400114; 3474122 |
| Can staff review their own restaurant? | No. "Conflict of interest" includes "current or former employment." Family members are also called out ("familial relationships"). Google's older wording was blunter: "If you own or work at a place, please don't review your own business or employer." | 7400114; historical wording via https://boast.io/google-review-policy-what-business-owners-can-and-cant-do-to-get-more-google-reviews/ |
| Bulk requests from a single device or location? | Reviews written on a shared device/kiosk are effectively banned: "Content that has been posted from multiple accounts by or at the request of one person" plus the "while on the premises" pressure ban. The older policy said "don't set up review stations or kiosks at your place of business." The NFC card is fine precisely because the guest uses their own phone and account. | 7400114; boast.io (historical text) |
| Many reviews from the same Wi-Fi/IP in a short window? | Not stated in policy text, but "unusual volumes or patterns of review contributions" is a stated removal ground, and practitioners report IP-based filtering: "Your customers share an IP address with someone who already left you a review"; "Your business offers WiFi that customers connect to and you've logged into your Google My Business account from this WiFi"; "You have an onsite review station (iPad, computer etc) at your location"; "The person who wrote the review works for you ... usually Google can trace this type of thing due to the fact that you share the same IP address." Google has not publicly confirmed IP tracking (a Local Search Forum thread reports a Google rep denying it, while practitioners see filtering). Treat it as a real risk. | 7400114; https://whitespark.ca/blog/google-remove-reviews/ ; https://localsearchforum.com/threads/google-reviews-coming-from-same-ip.53116/ |

### 1.5 What happens if a restaurant violates the policy
Google may apply, per https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287:
- "Business Profile will not be able to receive new reviews or ratings for set period of time"
- "Business Profile's existing reviews or ratings will be unpublished for set period of time"
- "Business Profile will display a warning to let consumers know that fake reviews were removed"
- "We will notify business owners via email if we plan to apply a restriction to their profile." Appeals are possible: "Once submitted, we will re-review the profile and any additional context the business provides in the appeal."

Consumers see (https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/15178562): "You may see a banner on the business's page to let you know that suspicious reviews were removed"; "You may not be able to post reviews or ratings on the place for a period of time"; "We may hide reviews for the business for a period of time."

### 1.6 Design implications for the NFC card program
- The card is an **invitation the guest controls**, delivered with the check, not a task the server waits for. Script: "If you'd like to leave us a review on Google, just tap your phone here whenever it's convenient, even later." Then walk away.
- **No incentive language anywhere** (card, table tent, menu, server script, social posts).
- **No "5-star" language** on the card or in scripts ("Tap for 5 stars" implies desired sentiment; see FTC §465.4 "conditioned expressly or by implication").
- **No names**: never "mention your server," no server leaderboards, no quotas, no per-server card codes that staff could use to compete. (Distinct URLs per table/section for analytics are not prohibited, but keep them invisible to staff incentives.)
- **Wi-Fi hygiene**: guests use their own mobile data; disable auto-join captive-portal prompts if possible; managers never log into GBP from the guest network; no staff reviews from any network.
- **Natural pacing**: Do not blast 60 tables on launch night. A sudden spike after months of nothing is exactly the "unusual volumes or patterns" pattern. Roll out over weeks.
- **Link target**: use the official write-review link. Third-party guides document `https://search.google.com/local/writereview?placeid=PLACE_ID` (Place ID from Google's Place ID Finder) or the "Get more reviews / Share review form" short link (g.page/.../review) from the Business Profile dashboard (https://support.reputation.com/s/article/How-do-I-make-a-direct-Google-Review-URL?language=en_US ; https://embedsocial.com/blog/google-review-link/). Point the NFC chip at your own HTTPS redirect domain with a 302 to the Google URL so you can re-point cards if Google changes URL formats (https://www.truereview.co/post/create-a-link-to-google-reviews-with-no-profile-access).

## 2. Federal law: the FTC Consumer Review Rule (16 CFR Part 465) and the Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255)

### 2.1 The Rule (effective October 21, 2024)
Announced Aug 14, 2024; published 89 FR 68077 (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/22/2024-18519/trade-regulation-rule-on-the-use-of-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials). FTC summary: it prohibits "selling or purchasing fake consumer reviews or testimonials, buying positive or negative consumer reviews, certain insiders creating consumer reviews or testimonials without clearly disclosing their relationships, creating a company-controlled review website that falsely purports to provide independent reviews, certain review suppression practices, and selling or purchasing fake indicators of social media influence" (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials).

**§465.2 Fake or false reviews** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.2): a business may not "write, create, or sell a consumer review ... that materially misrepresents, expressly or by implication: (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists; (2) That the reviewer ... used or otherwise had experience with the product, service, or business ...; or (3) The reviewer's ... experience." §465.2(c) extends this to procuring reviews "from its officers, managers, employees, or agents, or any of their immediate relatives, for posting on a third-party platform." Safe harbor §465.2(d): paragraphs (b) and (c) "do not apply to: (1) Reviews or testimonials that resulted from a business making generalized solicitations to purchasers to post reviews or testimonials about their experiences with the product, service, or business; or (2) Reviews that appear on a website or platform as a result of the business merely engaging in consumer review hosting." A card handed to every table is a "generalized solicitation to purchasers."

**§465.4 Buying positive or negative reviews** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.4), full text: "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to provide compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or conditioned expressly or by implication on, the writing or creation of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative, regarding the product, service, or business that is the subject of the review."

**§465.5 Insider reviews** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.5): "(a) ... for an officer or manager of a business to write or create a consumer review or consumer testimonial about the business ... that fails to have a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the officer's or manager's material relationship to the business ..." and (c) officers/managers may not solicit reviews from "immediate relatives, employees, or agents" without disclosure where they encouraged non-disclosure or knew and did not correct it. Definitions (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.1): "Officer" = "Owners, executives, and managing members of a business"; "Manager" = "An employee of a business who supervises other employees or agents and who either holds the title of a 'manager' or otherwise serves in a managerial role"; "Clear and conspicuous" = "easily noticeable (i.e., difficult to miss) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers."

**§465.7 Review suppression** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.7): (a) bans using "an unfounded or groundless legal threat, a physical threat, intimidation, or a public false accusation" to prevent or remove a review; (b) bans materially misrepresenting "that the consumer reviews ... represent most or all the reviews submitted" when reviews are suppressed by rating or negative sentiment. (Relevant if the restaurant ever displays reviews on its own site or if the founder builds a reviews widget: show them all or clearly label curation.)

**Penalties**: "up to $53,088 per violation" (FTC warning-letter press release Dec 22, 2025, https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-warns-10-companies-about-possible-violations-agencys-new-consumer-review-rule). The 2025 inflation adjustment set $53,088 (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-publishes-inflation-adjusted-civil-penalty-amounts-2025); no 2026 adjustment was made because October 2025 CPI data was not published during the shutdown (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/07/2026-13629/no-adjustment-to-civil-monetary-penalty-amounts).

### 2.2 FTC's own FAQ on the Rule (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- Q: "Can my business ask for reviews only from customers whom we think are happy with our services?" A: "The rule does not contain a specific prohibition against such conduct. But this practice could violate the FTC Act." (The FAQ points to Endorsement Guides §255.2(d) and (e)(11).)
- Q: "Is our business allowed to give consumers incentives for reviews?" A: "The rule does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there isn't an express or implied requirement that the reviews have to express a particular sentiment." (Note: Google *does* prohibit incentives, so this FTC latitude is irrelevant for Google reviews.)
- Q about a mass email to all customers where some were employees who reviewed without disclosing: "Your company didn't violate this section of the rule, which exempts generalized solicitations to purchasers..."
- Q: "I own a small business. Can I ask my family members to write consumer reviews of my business?" A: "Yes, but ask them to clearly and conspicuously disclose their relationship to your business." (Again, Google's conflict-of-interest rule is stricter; don't do it on Google.)
- Contacting negative reviewers to resolve issues and "simply asking satisfied customers to update their reviews" is not prohibited.

### 2.3 Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised July 26, 2023)
- §255.2(d) (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.2): "In procuring, suppressing, boosting, organizing, publishing, upvoting, downvoting, reporting, or editing consumer reviews of their products, advertisers should not take actions that have the effect of distorting or otherwise misrepresenting what consumers think of their products, regardless of whether the reviews are considered endorsements under the Guides."
- §255.2(e) Example 11: a marketer asks purchasers for feedback and "then invites purchasers who give very positive feedback to post online reviews of the products on third-party websites. Less pleased and unhappy purchasers are simply thanked for their feedback. Such a practice may be an unfair or deceptive practice if it results in the posted reviews being substantially more positive than if the marketer had not engaged in the practice." Inviting all purchasers equally is permissible even if the marketer expresses hope for favorable reviews.
- FTC's "What People Are Asking" FAQ (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking): sending review links only to high-satisfaction regions: "No. Only asking for reviews from customers who you think are more likely to be happy with your product would be misleading if it substantially skews the favorability of the reviews." Discount for saying good things: "No. ... You can't condition an incentive – for example, a discount – on a review being positive." Employees on review sites: "You definitely should disclose your employment relationship when writing such a review." Asking unhappy customers to change reviews: "You can also ask them if they'll add updates to their reviews. However, asking them to change or delete their initial negative reviews could mislead readers."
- Federal Register notice: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/07/26/2023-14795/guides-concerning-the-use-of-endorsements-and-testimonials-in-advertising

### 2.4 Enforcement climate (2025-2026)
- Dec 22, 2025: 10 warning letters; examples of conduct cited include "compensating employees for obtaining five-star reviews from friends and family and soliciting reviews from individuals who had no actual experience" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/12/warning-letter-or-ten-businesses-comply-ftcs-consumer-review-rule ; template letter https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2025-Fake-Review-Warning-Template.pdf).
- Apr-Jul 2026: TruHeight — employee-written reviews and free/discounted product for 5-star reviews; final order July 15, 2026, $4M judgment suspended on payment of $750,000 (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-approves-final-order-against-truheight-deceptive-unsubstantiated-advertising-supplements-kids).
- May 11, 2026: DOJ/FTC + Illinois AG civil-penalty suit against Premium Home Service for fake listings and fake five-star reviews by employees, relatives and SEO firms (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-illinois-take-action-stop-deceptive-conduct-company-created-thousands-business-listings-fake).
- Practitioner read: "enablers of fake reviews, like advertising and public relations companies and reputation management companies, are liable" under the Rule (https://www.dwt.com/insights/2024/11/ftc-faqs-on-consumer-review-testimonials-rule). As a review-solicitation vendor, the founder is in that category; the vendor's own marketing must never promise "more 5-star reviews."

## 3. The AI report naming individual servers

### 3.1 What is allowed
- Google's April 2026 rule bans the *merchant soliciting* reviews that identify staff. It does not ban guests from naming staff, and it does not restrict a business from reading its own reviews. "A customer who names your tech on their own is still fine" (https://digitalshiftmedia.com/google-review-policy-changes-2026-home-services/). The report may therefore summarize organic staff mentions.
- Reviews are public; reviewer names are public. Under California's CCPA, "personal information" excludes "publicly available" information, defined to include "Information that a business has a reasonable basis to believe is lawfully made available to the general public by the consumer or from widely distributed media" (Cal. Civ. Code §1798.140(v)(2), https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1798.140.&lawCode=CIV). Most state privacy laws have a similar carve-out and revenue thresholds far above this business.

### 3.2 The real risks (employment side)
1. **Using a review as evidence for discipline.** Guidance on lawful discipline stresses documentation, consistency, and investigation; vague or unverifiable feedback "is difficult to defend in court" (https://trustmineral.com/managing-employees/hidden-compliance-risks-lurking-in-your-performance-reviews/ ; https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/disciplining-your-workers-requires-a-legally-sound-policy). Guest reviews can be fake, mistaken about which server, or biased against protected characteristics; acting on them uncritically imports that bias into employment decisions.
2. **NLRA protected concerted activity.** Employees (union or not) cannot be disciplined for concerted complaints about working conditions, including public posts or contacting customers about conditions (https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/our-enforcement-activity/protected-concerted-activity). If a "review" is actually a staff member's complaint about scheduling/tips, discipline is risky.
3. **Tips.** Employers may not take employee tips except for a valid tip pool; a restaurant reported docking tips for insufficient online reviews is a wage-and-hour problem (https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/15-tipped-employees-flsa ; https://www.moneymade.com/careers/restaurant-taking-money-tips-employers). The report must never be tied to tip-outs or pay.
4. **AI-in-employment laws.** Illinois HB 3773 (effective Jan 1, 2026; applies to employers with one or more Illinois employees) requires notice to employees when AI is used in decisions about "discharge, discipline, tenure, or when the use could affect the terms, privileges, or conditions of employment," and prohibits discriminatory effect (https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-anti-discrimination-law-address-ai-goes-effect-1-january-2026 ; https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/legal-update-new-illinois-ai-law-requires-employee-notice-affirms-existing-employer-nondiscrimination-duties.html). Colorado's rewritten AI law (SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026) takes effect Jan 1, 2027, with a conditional exemption for employers with 40 or fewer employees (https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/06/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-ai-act ; https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/colorado-amends-its-artificial-intelligence-law-substantially-reducing). If the report is positioned as a management/performance tool, it can be an "AI used in employment decisions." Position it as a *guest-experience* report instead, and add the disclaimer below.
5. **Defamation exposure is low but real** if the report repeats a false factual accusation (theft, intoxication) about a named employee to third parties. Keep the report internal to the client, and quote rather than characterize.

### 3.3 HR-safe wording for the report (recommended boilerplate)
- Section title: "Guest comments that mention team members" (not "Server performance" or "Staff rankings").
- Present counts and verbatim quotes: "Guests mentioned 'Maria' in 6 reviews this month (5 positive, 1 negative). Sample: '...'" Do not compute per-server scores, rankings, or trends framed as performance metrics.
- Standing disclaimer printed on every report: "This section summarizes public guest comments on Google. Reviews are unverified, may be mistaken about who served the table, and are not an assessment of any employee's performance. Please verify with your own observations and your HR process before taking any personnel action. This report should not be used to determine pay, tips, scheduling, or discipline."
- Onboarding step for the restaurant: give staff a one-paragraph written notice: "We use a service that reads our public Google reviews and produces a monthly summary. Guest comments that mention team members by name are included so we can share praise and address service issues. This summary is not used to set pay or tips and is never the sole basis for discipline." (This also satisfies the transparency expectations under the Illinois law for clients there; confirm with counsel.)
- Offer an opt-out toggle: "Redact staff names in my report" for restaurants that prefer it.

## 4. Privacy and website legal pages

### 4.1 Do you need a Privacy Policy? Yes.
- **CalOPPA** applies to any commercial website collecting personally identifiable information from California residents, with no revenue threshold; it requires a conspicuously posted policy describing categories of PII collected, categories of third parties it is shared with, and how changes are communicated; enforcement via California's UCL at up to $2,500 per violation after a 30-day cure (https://termly.io/resources/articles/caloppa/ ; https://www.connmaciel.com/caloppa-privacy-policy-requirements/).
- **CCPA/CPRA** full obligations apply only above $26,625,000 revenue, 100,000 consumers, or 50% revenue from selling data (https://getuptocode.com/guides/ccpa-state-privacy-small-business).
- **Stripe requires it contractually**: "Business Users, as data controllers and/or the End Customer facing entity, are responsible for the contents of their privacy notice and cookie banner" and "Business Users are required to provide all necessary notices and obtain all necessary rights and consents from their End Customers to enable Stripe to lawfully collect, use, retain and disclose the Personal Data" (https://stripe.com/legal/privacy-center). Stripe's suggested paragraph: "We use Stripe for payments, analytics, and other business services. Stripe may collect personal data including via cookies and similar technologies. The personal data Stripe collects may include transactional data and identifying information about devices that connect to its services. Stripe uses this information to operate and improve the services it provides to us, including for fraud detection and prevention, authentication, analytics related to the performance of its services, and to enhance and customize the user experience. You can learn more about Stripe and read its privacy policy at https://stripe.com/privacy."
- **Cookies**: no US federal cookie-banner law; disclose analytics/Stripe cookies in the policy. Avoid session-replay/chat-widget tools without disclosure: California CIPA suits carry $5,000 statutory damages per violation and are filed against businesses of every size (https://www.thenerdstack.com/posts/state-privacy-laws-small-business-websites).

### 4.2 What the Privacy Policy should cover (for this business)
1. Data collected on the website: lead-form fields (name, restaurant, email, phone), order/billing data (via Stripe; you never store card numbers), analytics cookies.
2. Data collected to deliver the report: the restaurant's Google Business Profile data accessed with its authorization (reviews, reviewer display names, ratings, dates, owner replies); state that review content is public information published by reviewers on Google, that you process it to produce the client's report, that raw API content is retained no more than 30 days (see §5.2), and that reports are shared only with the client.
3. Third parties: Stripe, hosting, email provider, the AI/LLM provider used to write the report (name it; state that reviews are sent to it for processing), analytics.
4. Rights/requests, contact email, effective date, change notice, children (not directed to under-13s).
5. Marketing email: you will email leads; provide unsubscribe (CAN-SPAM basics — general guidance, not researched here).

### 4.3 Terms of Service — what they must cover
Stripe's website checklist (https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/checklist/website) lists what every Stripe merchant's site should show: description of what you sell, purchase currency, customer-service contact "something besides contact forms," fulfillment policies ("Refund policy ... Shipping or delivery policy ... Return policy and process ... Cancellation policy: Describe the conditions under which customers can cancel subscriptions or reservations"), privacy policy, business address, "the terms of any promotions you're offering," PCI/HTTPS statement, card logos. Stripe's Services Terms also require a "fair and neutral refund and exchange policy" (https://stripe.com/legal/ssa-services-terms). Stripe Checkout can display your ToS/Privacy links and require agreement ("Display agreement to legal terms") (https://docs.stripe.com/payments/checkout/customization/policies).

Subscription-specific (federal ROSCA, 15 U.S.C. §8403, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403): for online negative-option (auto-renewing) charges the seller must (1) "clearly and conspicuously disclose all material terms of the transaction before obtaining" billing information, (2) "obtain a consumer's express informed consent before charging," and (3) "provide simple mechanisms for a consumer to stop recurring charges." The FTC's 2024 "Click-to-Cancel" rule was challenged in court and the FTC opened a new Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking in March 2026 (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/negative-option-rule); many states (California's ARL among them) impose similar and sometimes stricter auto-renewal disclosure and online-cancellation rules. Practical: show "$50/month, renews monthly until cancelled, cancel anytime from your account or by email" at checkout with an unticked consent checkbox, and let the Stripe Customer Portal handle cancellation.

Also include in the ToS:
- What the subscription includes (monthly report, 10 replacement cards/month while subscribed; unused replacements do not roll over, or state your rule), card shipping times, defective-card replacement, what happens to cards if the subscription lapses (they keep working; NFC links stay live — decide and state this).
- **No-guarantee clause**: you do not guarantee any number of reviews, any rating, or that Google will publish any review; Google may filter reviews for reasons outside your control.
- **Client compliance covenant**: the restaurant agrees not to offer incentives, gate reviews, pressure guests on-premises, set staff quotas, or ask guests to name staff, and acknowledges Google may restrict its profile if it does. This is your liability shield as a "reputation management" vendor under the FTC Rule.
- **Authorization**: the client authorizes you to access its Business Profile (as Manager or via OAuth) to read reviews and (optionally) draft replies; you will not post replies without approval (Google API policy: "If you respond to reviews on behalf of your end-client, you must receive their authorization first").
- **AI disclaimer**: reports are AI-generated summaries that may contain errors; not professional, legal, or HR advice; staff-mention disclaimer (see §3.3).
- **Client logo license**: client grants you a license to print its name/logo on cards and represents it owns them.
- **Trademark statement**: "Google, the Google logo and Google Maps are trademarks of Google LLC. [Company] is not affiliated with, sponsored by, or endorsed by Google."
- Limitation of liability, governing law, dispute resolution.

### 4.4 ADA / accessibility for the website
- There is no DOJ Title III web rule; the DOJ's 2024 rule (WCAG 2.1 AA) applies to state/local governments (Title II), and its deadlines were pushed to 2027/2028 in April 2026; Title III rulemaking is paused (https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2025-august/digital-accessibility-under-title-iii-ada/ ; https://www.adatitleiii.com/).
- Private suits continue: 3,117 federal web-accessibility suits in 2025 (+27% YoY), over 5,000 including state courts; courts use WCAG 2.1 AA as the benchmark, WCAG 2.2 AA is best practice (https://www.levelaccess.com/blog/2024-u-s-web-accessibility-litigation-key-trends-and-strategies-for-mitigating-risk/). There is no small-business exemption; most suits start with a $10k-$25k demand letter (https://ideaforgestudios.com/2026/07/15/ada-website-compliance-for-small-business-what-the-2025-lawsuit-surge-actually-m/).
- Overlay widgets are not a defense; an overlay vendor was ordered by the FTC to pay $1 million in April 2025 for false compliance claims (https://www.inclusiveweb.co/accessibility-resources/how-to-protect-your-business-from-ada-website-lawsuits-in-2026).
- Build checklist: semantic HTML, alt text, keyboard-navigable checkout and forms with visible focus, 4.5:1 contrast, labeled inputs, captions on any demo video, no auto-playing motion, an accessibility statement page with a contact email. Run axe/Lighthouse before launch.

## 5. The NFC card and data pipeline: disclosure, security, and data-source rules

### 5.1 NFC card disclosures
- No US law or Google policy was found that requires specific disclosure text on an NFC card pointing to Google. The practical consumer-protection point is that the guest should be able to see where the tap leads. On iPhone, background tag reading shows a banner and "the user must tap the notification in order to avoid performing an action accidentally"; only URL records are supported (https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corenfc/adding-support-for-background-tag-reading). Print the destination domain (e.g., "opens review.yourdomain.com → Google") and a QR fallback for phones without background NFC reading (https://www.mobilocard.com/post/are-nfc-business-cards-safe).
- Security hygiene recommended by NFC vendors: lock tags read-only after encoding, HTTPS-only destination on a domain you control, ability to re-point/deactivate a card remotely, periodic inspection for tampered cards (https://tapt.io/en-us/blogs/news/nfc-business-card-security-privacy-guide).
- FCC: passive NFC tags contain no transmitter; I did not verify the certification status and list it as an open question (low risk, but confirm with the card supplier's compliance sheet).

### 5.2 How the report may lawfully get the reviews (this shapes the product)
- **Scraping is out.** Google Maps Platform Terms: "(a) No Scraping. Customer will not export, extract, or otherwise scrape Google Maps Content for use outside of the Services" (https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms); Google's general ToS bars automated harvesting. Third-party scrapers are a ToS breach even where courts have found public-data scraping lawful; reviews are also copyright-protected user content.
- **Places API is insufficient**: "You must not pre-fetch, cache, or store Places API content beyond the allowed exceptions" and it returns only 5 reviews per place (https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/places/web-service/policies ; https://featurable.com/blog/google-places-more-than-5-reviews).
- **Business Profile API is the sanctioned path**: `accounts.locations.reviews.list` "is only valid if the specified location is verified" (https://developers.google.com/my-business/reference/rest/v4/accounts.locations.reviews/list). Access must be requested; requirements include "a Google Business Profile that is verified and active for 60+ days" and "a website representing the business listed on the GBP"; quota 0 QPM = not approved, 300 QPM = approved (https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/prereqs). Approval reportedly takes weeks (https://localith.ai/blog/google-business-profile-api-guide/).
- **Data retention rule** (https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/policies): "You cannot pre-fetch, cache, index, or store any content provided through the Business Profile APIs ('Content') for use outside of your Business Profile project except for limited amounts of Content." Stored Content "must be stored temporarily for no more than 30 calendar days," "must be stored securely," and "cannot be manipulated or aggregated in any way." Also: "If you respond to reviews on behalf of your end-client, you must receive their authorization first," and account changes must be notified to the end-client within 48 hours.
  - Design consequence: pull reviews fresh each month via the client's authorization, generate the report, retain the *report* (your derived work) but purge raw review text within 30 days; do not build a permanent multi-restaurant review database. Whether an AI summary counts as "aggregation" of stored content is an open legal question — get counsel's read, and keep the raw corpus ephemeral to stay conservative.
- **Zero-code fallback for the local test run**: the restaurant adds the founder as a **Manager** on its Business Profile; the founder reads reviews in the dashboard and copies them into the AI tool manually each month. Slower, but fully within Google's rules for a 5-10 restaurant pilot while API access is pending.
- **Ownership of review text**: Google's ToS says "Your content remains yours" and Google gets a license to "host, reproduce, distribute, communicate, and use your content" including to "quote a review you wrote" (https://policies.google.com/terms). Google's brand guidance adds: "You must get consent from the reviewer if you want to use customer reviews of your business for your own marketing purposes, such as on your website or in print or digital ads" (https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/use-cases/customer-reviews/). Internal analysis for the business is not marketing; quoting reviews on the restaurant's website or in ads needs reviewer consent.

## 6. Trademarks: using "Google," the G logo, "Review us on Google," the Maps pin, stars

### 6.1 The explicit use-case guidance (https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/use-cases/customer-reviews/)
**Ratings**: "Describe your rating as being 'on Google.'" "Include an 'as of' date when sharing your overall rating or number of reviews." "Don't imply that ratings come from Google. For example, don't use terms like 'Google-rated' or 'Google rating.'" "Don't add stars by the Google name or logos."
**Reviews**: "Include one of our logos (either the Google G or full Google wordmark)." "Make sure all logos are the same size if you're using our logo in a group of other companies' logos." "Don't use any unofficial logos, badges, or lockups." "Get consent from reviewers to use their reviews in your marketing."

### 6.2 General brand rules (https://about.google/brand-resource-center/guidance/)
- Without a request you may "refer to Google or our products in an informational context in plain text" and link to Google content "using hyperlinks, embeds, or QR codes."
- Never "use, incorporate, or combine any of our trademarks (names or logos) into your brand name, product name, business name, trade name, website domain, or slogan." (So the new company name/domain must not contain "Google," "GTap," a G-lookalike, etc.)
- Do not imply endorsement; do not imitate Google's visual identity; do not use Google brand elements "on merchandise or in contests."
- Logo specifics (https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/branding-guidelines/how-to-show-googles-brand/): clear space equal to the width of the G; "Use the full-color Google logo whenever possible. Stick to white or black backgrounds"; "Only use the newest version of the Google G (the one with blended gradient colors)"; "Don't combine your logo with the Google G or modify the Google G in any way, including changing the color." Official EPS/PNG/TIFF/JPG files (RGB and CMYK) are downloadable there.
- Terms (https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google/trademarks-and-terms/terms-and-conditions/): "Any use of the Google Brand Features must be accompanied by a notice that clearly indicates that the Google Brand Features are trademarks or distinctive brand features of Google LLC." Permission is limited to the approved materials and "Google reserves the right in its sole discretion to terminate or modify your permission."

### 6.3 Maps pin and 5-star graphics
- The "Google Maps red pin element" is listed as a Google trademark (https://about.google/brand-resource-center/products-and-services/geo-guidelines/) and Google holds design patents on the teardrop marker (D661704, D620950, D621413, D645052 cited at https://www.avvo.com/legal-answers/google-maps-pin-3165791.html). Do not print the red pin on cards.
- Stars: the guidance bans stars beside the Google name or logo. A generic five-star row elsewhere on the card is not a trademark problem but is a *policy* problem: "Tap for 5 stars" reads as soliciting a particular sentiment. Avoid star graphics entirely on the card; use them only on the founder's own website in a neutral context.
- Google's former Marketing Kit ("Review us on Google" stickers/postcards) went offline in 2024 per https://localimpact.com/blog/review-us-on-google-sticker (medium confidence); there is no official Google-supplied card art to rely on.

### 6.4 Recommended card and website wording
Safe (restaurant is the speaker; Google's role stated accurately):
- "Tap to review us on Google" + Google G (official file, full color, clear space) + tiny line: "Google and the Google logo are trademarks of Google LLC."
- "Enjoyed your visit? Leave us an honest review on Google — tap your phone here, now or later."
- "Your feedback helps us get better. Tap to open our Google review page."
Avoid: "Google-rated," "5-star restaurant," "Tap for 5 stars," "Google review card" as a product name, any Google mark inside your own logo, the red pin, star rows next to the G, colored/monochrome G variants.

Because the founder sells the printed cards (arguably "merchandise") and is a third party rather than the restaurant, submit the card template via Google's permission request (https://www.google.com/permissions) before mass printing. Prepare a plain-text fallback design ("Review us on Google" set in your own typeface, no logo), which needs no permission at all. On the founder's website, refer to Google in plain text, show the G only in a product mock-up with the trademark notice, and state non-affiliation in the footer.

## 7. Sales tax (general guidance; varies by state and changes often)

- 45 states levy statewide sales tax; Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon do not; combined rates run up to 10.11% (Louisiana) as of Jan 1, 2026 (https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/sales-tax-rates/).
- **Physical cards** are tangible personal property and are taxable in essentially every sales-tax state. Physical presence in the founder's home state creates nexus there immediately: register for a seller's permit and collect on every in-state sale (including the local pilot restaurants) from day one.
- **Out-of-state card sales**: economic nexus after Wayfair; the standard threshold is $100,000 of sales into a state over 12 months (41 of 46 jurisdictions), with $500,000 in California, New York and Texas and $250,000 in Alabama and Mississippi; 17 states have dropped the 200-transaction test as of Aug 1, 2026, but some retain it (https://taxcloud.com/blog/sales-tax-nexus-by-state/ ; https://www.taxjar.com/sales-tax/economic-nexus). Below thresholds you generally have no collection duty in that state.
- **The $50/month report (SaaS or "information/data-processing service")**: taxable in some form in 25-26 jurisdictions in 2026 (e.g., NY, TX at 80% of the charge as data processing, PA, MA, WA, OH, CT at 1% for business use, MD unless solely commercial use), exempt in others (e.g., FL, GA, CA until 2027, IL, NJ, VA) — sources disagree on several states (AL, IN, OH, SC) (https://taxcloud.com/blog/saas-sales-tax-by-state/ ; https://www.anrok.com/saas-sales-tax-by-state ; https://thetaxvalet.com/blog/which-states-require-sales-tax-on-software-as-a-service). California begins taxing electronically delivered prewritten software Jan 1, 2027, and Colorado's June 4, 2026 law expands taxable software to remote access from Jan 1, 2027 (https://www.claconnect.com/en/resources/articles/26/saas-sales-tax-california-colorado). Chicago's 9% lease-transaction tax applies to SaaS locally (https://www.numeral.com/blog/sales-tax-on-saas).
- **Bundling trap**: if the invoice bundles cards (taxable) with the subscription (maybe exempt), many states tax the whole bundle. Itemize cards and subscription separately, and treat the "10 free replacement cards" as a no-charge line item.
- Use Stripe Tax (or TaxJar/Avalara) with product tax codes for "physical goods" and "SaaS - business use," and confirm the home-state treatment of the report with a CPA before the first invoice.

## 8. DO / DON'T list for the restaurant onboarding guide

**DO**
- Offer the card to every table, every time, regardless of how the meal went. Universal ask = no gating.
- Present it as optional: "If you'd like to leave us a review on Google, tap here — now or later." Then leave the table.
- Let guests use their own phone, their own Google account, and their own mobile data.
- Reply to every review, positive and negative, politely and without personal information (Google: "Never share a reviewer's private information or engage in personal attacks").
- Give staff the one-paragraph notice that public reviews are summarized monthly and that name mentions are shared for praise and coaching only.
- Verify anything in a review before acting on it; treat the monthly report's staff section as guest opinion, not evidence.
- Keep a private feedback channel (email/QR to a form) for *everyone*, offered alongside the Google card, not instead of it.
- Roll the cards out gradually across weeks, not all tables on night one.
- Keep the restaurant's Business Profile logins off the guest Wi-Fi.
- Keep Google's name in plain text or the official G with the trademark line; never alter it.

**DON'T**
- Don't offer anything for a review: no discount, free dessert, drink, raffle, loyalty points, or "show us your review" perks. Not even for honest reviews. Google removes them and can restrict your profile.
- Don't ask only happy guests, and don't route unhappy guests away from Google to a private form first.
- Don't wait at the table, hover, or say "could you do it before you go?" Pressure on the premises is a stated violation.
- Don't set review quotas or contests for servers, and don't ask guests to "mention [server] by name."
- Don't hand guests a house tablet or phone, and don't tap the card to a staff device for them.
- Don't let staff, owners, family, suppliers or friends review the restaurant. Ever.
- Don't ask a reviewer to delete or change a negative review; you may resolve the issue and invite them to add an update.
- Don't threaten reviewers or post accusations in replies.
- Don't dock tips, pay, or shifts based on reviews or the monthly report.
- Don't put "5-star," "Google-rated," or star graphics next to the Google logo on anything.

## 9. Staff training card (fits on the back of a card)

1. Drop the card with the check at every table. Same words every time: "If you'd like to leave us an honest review on Google, just tap your phone on this — now or whenever." Walk away.
2. Never mention your name, ask for stars, or promise anything for a review.
3. If a guest asks how: "Hold the top of your phone on the card; a link to our Google page pops up. Use your own data, not our Wi-Fi."
4. If a guest complains: fix it now, tell a manager. Do not steer them away from the card.
5. You, your friends and your family don't review us on Google. It's against Google's rules and will get real reviews deleted.
6. If it's not working on their phone: point to the QR code on the card.

## 1. Google Business Profile third-party policies (vendor obligations and the "add me as Manager" fallback)

- Exit: "Within 7 business days of notice from an end customer, you must provide that client the ability to disassociate the Google Account used to manage their Business Profile from your services."
- Enforcement: compliance reviews at any time, corrective-action requests, then suspension of third-party program participation or the account; "In cases of serious or repeated violations, they may act immediately and without notification."

The API policy page repeats the same duties for API users (authorization before replying, credential integrity, 7-business-day disassociation) at https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/policies. The "authorized representative" rules in the eligibility guidelines add: "Never claim a Business Profile without express consent from the business owner" and "Always work directly with the business owner to complete verification" (https://support.google.com/business/answer/13763036).

**Is the Manager fallback compliant?** Yes, if done this way: (1) the restaurant owner adds the founder's Google account as **Manager** (never Owner; never a shared login) from People and access; Google's help confirms managers "have mostly the same access to the profile as owners" except adding/removing users and removing the profile (https://support.google.com/business/answer/3403100); (2) the pilot agreement contains a checkbox or signature granting express written consent to access the profile and read reviews, plus a separate checkbox if the founder will ever post replies; (3) the agreement promises disassociation within 7 business days of notice and that no profile edits will be made without the owner's consent; (4) the founder's marketing never states or implies a guaranteed review count, rating, or ranking. What to disclose in onboarding: that Business Profile itself is free, exactly what access is used for, that Google may restrict profiles that violate review policy, and that the founder will remove himself on request.

## 2. Consumer Review Fairness Act (15 U.S.C. §45b) and restaurant form contracts

Text at https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45b. A "form contract" is one "with standardized terms ... used by a person in the course of selling or leasing the person's goods or services; and imposed on an individual without a meaningful opportunity for such individual to negotiate." A "covered communication" is "a written, oral, or pictorial review, performance assessment of, or other similar analysis of ... the goods, services, or conduct of a person by an individual who is party to a form contract." A form-contract provision is void and unlawful if it (1) "prohibits or restricts the ability" of a party to post a covered communication, (2) "imposes a penalty or fee" for doing so, or (3) requires transfer of intellectual property in the review beyond "a non-exclusive license." Businesses may still remove or bar content that is defamatory, harassing, obscene, discriminatory, unrelated to the goods, "clearly false or misleading," or that discloses confidential/trade-secret/medical information (FTC guide: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-review-fairness-act-what-businesses-need-know). Violations are "treated the same as violating an FTC rule defining an unfair or deceptive act," enforceable by the FTC and state attorneys general.

**DON'T-list addition for restaurants:** never put a "no negative reviews," "no social media posts," "review = forfeited deposit," or "we own your review" clause in reservation, large-party deposit, private-event, catering, gift-card or online-ordering terms. It is void anyway and creates FTC/AG exposure, and it dovetails with 16 CFR 465.7 (review suppression) already in the main report.

## 6. The redirect endpoint: guest data you will collect and what to do about it

The main report's architecture (chip -> your HTTPS domain -> 302 -> Google) means your server sees each guest's IP, user agent and timestamp. Status of the risk as of today:

- **CIPA §638.51 "pen register" suits**: the Legislature passed **SB 690** on Aug 28, 2026 (Assembly 66-0, Senate unanimous). If signed (governor deadline reported as Sept 30, 2026; effective Jan 1, 2027), it removes the private right of action for §638.51 website/app claims and applies retroactively to pending actions commenced on or after Jan 1, 2025; the AG remains the enforcer. It does **not** touch CIPA §§631/632 (wiretap/eavesdrop), CDAFA, ECPA, VPPA or common-law privacy claims (https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/alerts-technology-cldr-california-curbs-cipa-pen-register-suits).
- **Variety Media v. Superior Court** (Cal. Ct. App., tentative ruling Aug 24, 2026, argued Aug 25): a visitor's IP address "identifies the source of a communication rather than its destination," so collecting it alone is not pen-register activity; the court left open whether it is trap-and-trace activity (https://www.carpedatumlaw.com/2026/08/california-court-of-appeal-tentatively-holds-that-collecting-a-website-visitors-ip-address-alone-does-not-constitute-pen-register-activity-under-cipa/). Roughly 4,000 CIPA suits were on file in California as of July 2026, and demand letters target businesses of every size (https://www.insideprivacy.com/state-privacy/california-legislature-advances-bill-targeting-wave-of-cipa-pen-register-lawsuits/ ; https://news.designrush.com/cipa-demand-letters-small-business-cookiebot).
- **Texas TDPSA** does not apply to an SBA-defined small business except for the ban on selling sensitive data without consent (https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/07/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act-overview). **CalOPPA** still requires the privacy policy to describe what the hop collects (main report §4.1).

**Recommended pilot configuration (no source says the law requires this; it is risk minimization):**
1. Make the hop a bare 302 with no HTML, no cookies, no JavaScript, no third-party pixels or analytics scripts. That removes the fact patterns (third-party trackers, session replay, form capture) that drive the surviving §631 theories.
2. Log only what you need for analytics: card/table ID + coarse timestamp. Do not store IP or full user agent; if your host logs them by default, truncate IPs (/24) or hash with a rotating salt and set retention to **no more than 30 days** (mirrors the Google API and LLM-vendor 30-day windows so one retention statement covers everything).
3. State in the privacy policy: "When you tap a card, our server records that the card was used and forwards you to Google. We do not identify you. Standard connection data is deleted within 30 days." A notice cannot practically be shown during a 302, so print the destination on the card ("opens review.[domain] -> Google") as the main report already suggests.
4. Consider disabling per-tap logging entirely for the pilot; table-level counts can be inferred from Google review counts.

**Anthropic (Claude API)** (https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention ; https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/8956058-i-have-a-zero-data-retention-agreement-with-anthropic-what-products-does-it-apply-to): under the Commercial Terms, "Retained data is never used for model training without your express permission"; conversation content "is not retained by default"; standard commercial retention deletes inputs/outputs within 30 days; **Zero Data Retention** is requested through sales and enabled per organization; flagged content may be kept up to 2 years. Important exception: Claude Fable 5/5.1 and Mythos 5/5.1 are "Covered Models" that "require 30-day data retention and are not available under ZDR unless expressly authorized by Anthropic."

**Is sending review content to the LLM "use outside of your Business Profile project"?** The API policy (https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/policies) says you may store limited Content "only to improve the performance of your project," for no more than 30 calendar days, "stored securely," and "cannot be manipulated or aggregated in any way." It does not address subprocessors. A vendor that processes the client's reviews to deliver the client's report is arguably acting within the project's purpose, but nothing in the policy expressly permits transferring Content to a third-party processor, so this is a counsel question. Both vendors' default 30-day windows and no-training terms satisfy the "temporary" and "secure" prongs numerically; ZDR satisfies them more cleanly. Mitigations: strip reviewer display names before sending (the report never needs them), send only review text/rating/date, sign the vendor DPA, name the provider in the privacy policy, and prefer the Takeout data path in §8, where the API policy does not apply at all.

## 8 and 16. Can the monthly report and "trends vs last month" be built lawfully from Business Profile data?

**Status of the Reviews API:** Google's review-data page and deprecation schedule show `accounts.locations.reviews.list` (v4) is live and still receiving features ("You can now view the specific policy violations for any of your review replies"); the deprecated items are reportInsights, Business Calls, and location association methods, not reviews (https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/review-data ; https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/sunset-dates). One vendor blog claims the reviews API was "deprecated for new projects in 2024"; Google's own pages contradict this. The real barrier is quota approval (0 QPM until approved).

**Eligibility applies to the founder's own business.** The access application requires "a Google Business Profile that is verified and active for 60+ days" and "a website representing the business listed on the GBP" (https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/prereqs). Action item: create and verify the founder's own Business Profile and put the website live on day one so the 60-day clock starts now; approval reportedly takes further weeks.

**Reconciling the policy text with the product.** The 30-day/no-aggregation rule governs stored raw API "Content." A generated report is a derived work, and month-over-month comparison of your own derived metrics (counts, themes, sentiment) does not require retaining raw Content past 30 days. The policy does not expressly bless derived works, so a conservative design is: pull fresh each month, generate, store only the report and derived metrics, purge raw text and reviewer identifiers within 30 days, never build a cross-client raw review database.

**Alternative lawful data path that avoids the API policy entirely: Google Takeout.** An Owner or Manager of the profile can export "Business Profile" data from takeout.google.com; the archive contains one `reviews.json` per location with reviewer display names, star ratings, review text, dates and owner replies (JSON only, no CSV) (https://help.reviewflowz.com/en/articles/13161174-how-to-export-google-reviews-to-excel ; https://www.truereview.co/post/export-google-my-business-reviews ; https://saijogeorge.com/export-google-reviews/). Because this is the account holder's own export under Google's general Terms ("Your content remains yours"), the API-specific storage restrictions do not apply textually; reviewer copyright and Google's marketing-consent rule still do, but internal analysis for the business is not marketing. There is no "download all reviews" button in the dashboard. Review-notification emails are a third, slower fallback. Scraping remains prohibited (Maps Platform Terms; main report §5.2).

**Recommended pilot pipeline:** Manager access (with written consent per §1) -> monthly Takeout export by the founder -> strip reviewer names -> LLM (ZDR if available) -> report; keep month-to-month derived metrics; retain the raw JSON only as long as the client authorizes in the pilot agreement (30 days is a clean default). Move to the API once approved.

## 9. Marketing-claims compliance for the founder's website and pilot testimonials

- **Material connections** (16 CFR 255.5(a), https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5): "When there exists a connection between the endorser and the seller of the advertised product that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously." Free or discounted product is expressly a material connection (Example 7, the free lathe). Pilot restaurants that got free demo cards and give testimonials therefore need a disclosure **in or immediately next to** the testimonial ("Pilot participant; received free cards"), not in a footer (Example 3(i): closing-credit placement is not clear and conspicuous). The advertiser is liable for the endorser's omissions (§255.1).
- **Performance claims** ("X% more reviews," "3x reviews"): objective claims need a "reasonable basis" before dissemination (FTC Policy Statement on Advertising Substantiation, https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation). The FTC's stated view on "up to" claims is that consumers should be "likely to achieve the maximum results promised under normal circumstances" (Arise settlement discussion, https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-settlement-illustrates-challenges-with-up-to-claims); "results not typical" disclaimers do not cure atypical testimonials (Endorsement Guides §255.2). Practical: publish only pilot data you actually measured, state the sample ("across 6 pilot restaurants over 8 weeks"), and never promise ratings.
- **Company-controlled review sites** (16 CFR 465.6, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.6): it is unlawful "to materially misrepresent ... that a website, organization, or entity that it controls, owns, or operates provides independent reviews or opinions." If you ever build a reviews widget or "best restaurants in [city]" page, label it as yours and, if you display client reviews, show them all or label the curation (465.7(b)).

## 10. Review velocity and timing: practitioner numbers

Google publishes no thresholds; its help page says only "Reviews are checked to ensure they comply with our policies. In some cases, this process might take a few days" and "Reviews removed for policy violations won't be restored" (https://support.google.com/business/answer/10313341). Practitioner heuristics (2025-2026, all secondary):

- Filtering keys on deviation from the profile's own baseline, not an absolute number: "if five reviews come in within ten minutes from a business that usually gets one a week, the algorithm assumes manipulation" (https://wiserreview.com/blog/google-review-velocity/).
- Red-flag patterns cited: 20+ reviews in 48 hours; a jump from ~0/week to 25/week for two weeks then back to 0; 30 in a single week after silence (https://sproutsagesolutions.com/review-velocity-vs-review-count/ ; https://www.plumbdev.com/seo-services-review-velocity ; https://www.epicware.ai/blog/review-velocity-vs-total-review-count).
- "Safe" pacing advice: 2-5 new reviews per week for a small business, "never more than 1 or 2 per day"; if a batch was filtered, wait 10-14 days before re-asking (https://reviewgrow.com/blog/why-are-my-google-reviews-not-showing-up ; https://localo.com/blog/review-velocity-study).
- Publication lag: typically 1-24 hours; 3-7 days when held for checks, new accounts, or bursts (https://praising.ai/blog/how-long-google-reviews-take-to-show-up ; https://birdeye.com/blog/review-not-showing-up-on-google/).
- Timing after the visit: for local services the window is "a few days"; on-the-spot NFC/QR asks convert far better than next-day email (case studies: 3% -> 31% for a restaurant switching from a 3-day-later email to a checkout QR; 6% -> 47% for a salon NFC chip at reception) (https://www.business-ereputation.com/en/when-should-you-ask-your-customers-for-a-google-review/ ; https://www.business-ereputation.com/en/the-perfect-time-to-ask-for-a-google-review-psychological-timing-and-response-rate/).

**Reconciling "fresh memory" with "now or later":** the script is about pressure, not timing; guests who tap at the table still produce same-day reviews. The quantitative risk is volume: a 100-cover night at even 5% conversion is 5 reviews/night, ~35/week, which is a spike for a profile that historically got 1-2/week. So the rollout rule should be numeric: week 1, cards at a quarter of tables or one shift; raise weekly, targeting no more than roughly double the prior month's weekly review rate at each step, and treat 20+ in 48 hours as the ceiling to avoid regardless of size. These are heuristics, not Google policy; say so to clients.

## 11. Employee-record law, redaction, and staff requests

- **California Labor Code §1198.5**: employees and former employees may inspect and copy "personnel records that the employer maintains relating to the employee's performance or to any grievance concerning the employee" within 30 calendar days of a written request; employers may redact "the name of any nonsupervisory employee" (not customer names); $750 penalty plus injunctive relief and fees (https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/faq_righttoinspectpersonnelfiles.htm). Practitioners say complaints about the employee "would likely be covered" (https://www.calpeculiarities.com/2016/12/06/the-peculiar-personnel-ity-of-california-personnel-file-inspection-laws/).
- **Illinois Personnel Record Review Act, 820 ILCS 40/2** (amended eff. Jan 1, 2025): employees may inspect "any personnel documents which are, have been or are intended to be used in determining that employee's qualifications for employment, promotion, transfer, compensation, benefits, discharge, or other disciplinary action"; the employer "shall comply ... within 7 working days after the receipt of the request" (+7 calendar days if shown necessary); at least 2 requests per year (https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/082000400K2.htm ; https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/latest-updates-illinois-personnel-records-review-act). **820 ILCS 40/4**: personnel record information "not included in the personnel record ... shall not be used by an employer in a judicial or quasi-judicial proceeding" (https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/082000400K4.htm). Consequence: a restaurant that wants to rely on a report for discipline must place it in the file, at which point the employee can read it.
- **Decisions for the product:** (a) redact **reviewer** display names in the report by default (data minimization; the analysis never needs them; it also removes the personal-data transfer to the LLM); (b) staff names: keep the main report's disclaimer and opt-out toggle, and add a per-employee removal path ("a team member may ask the restaurant to have their name redacted from future reports"); no statute gives staff a right to demand removal from an internal report, but honoring requests reduces NLRA and discrimination exposure; (c) tell clients in writing that if a report is used for any employment decision it becomes an inspectable personnel record in CA/IL and similar states.

## 12. Physical-goods compliance for the cards

- **Prop 65.** DEHP (a common PVC plasticizer) is listed for cancer and reproductive toxicity (https://www.p65warnings.ca.gov/fact-sheets/di2-ethylhexyl-dehp). Private enforcers have served notices on PVC ID holders and bracelets for DEHP (https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/prop65/notices/2021-01359.pdf ; https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/prop65/notices/2020-01196.pdf); settlements commonly use a 0.1% (1,000 ppm) per-phthalate reformulation benchmark. Businesses with fewer than 10 employees are exempt from the warning duty, but penalties for others run up to $2,500 per violation per day (https://www.compliancegate.com/california-proposition-65/). Short-form warnings must name at least one chemical for products manufactured on or after Jan 1, 2028 (https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/04/prop-65-changes-to-short-form-warnings-will-cause-long-term-impacts/). **Recommendation:** specify phthalate-free PVC, PETG or wood cards and get the supplier's test report or declaration (<0.1% DEHP/DBP/BBP/DIDP/DnHP); otherwise print the DEHP warning on packaging and the product page.
- **Country-of-origin marking** (19 U.S.C. 1304; 19 CFR Part 134, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/part-134): every imported article "or its container" must be marked "Made in China"/"Product of China" legibly and permanently; "Imported from China" is not acceptable. §134.46: if a US city or state name appears on the imported article, "Made in China" must appear in close proximity and comparable size, which matters if cards are printed abroad with the restaurant's address. Non-compliance: 10% marking duty and possible §1592 penalties (https://www.peacocktariffconsulting.com/country-of-origin-marking-rules/). Simplest path: import blank cards in cartons marked "Made in China," print/encode in the US, and keep the marking on retail packaging.
- **Tariff.** NFC cards are HTS **8523.52.00** "smart cards" (CBP rulings NY N303172 and NY N083108); general rate **Free** (https://hts.usitc.gov/ ; https://www.customsmobile.com/rulings/docview?doc_id=NY+N303172). Chinese-origin smart cards fall under Section 301 List 3 (9903.88.03) at **25%**. The IEEPA fentanyl/reciprocal tariffs were struck down Feb 20, 2026 (https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/supreme-court-strikes-down-ieepa-tariffs); the replacement Section 122 10% global tariff ran Feb 24 - Jul 24, 2026 and was succeeded on Jul 24 by new country-level Section 301 duties of "10 or 12.5 percent" (91 FR 47318, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/28/2026-15181/notice-of-actions-in-section-301-investigations-of-acts-policies-and-practices-of-various-economies ; https://gingercontrol.com/blog/section-122-tariffs-explained). Expect roughly 35-37.5% total on Chinese cards; the exact China rate under the July 2026 notice was not verified, and the $800 de minimis exemption no longer applies to Chinese goods, so confirm with a customs broker before ordering. Vietnam/Taiwan-origin cards avoid List 3.
- **FCC (answering the open question):** passive tags are not certified individually. FCC OET guidance: "A passive tag does not contain batteries and is not certified individually. Only the tag reader needs to be tested," subject to not operating in a restricted band (https://transition.fcc.gov/oet/ea/presentations/files/may05/New_Policies_Pt._15_SD.pdf ; https://transition.fcc.gov/oet/ea/presentations/files/oct07/Oct_07-Pt_15_Updates&New_Interps-JD.pdf). Some tag vendors market "FCC IDs" for passive tags; that is not a regulatory requirement.

## 13. Sales-tax gaps

- **Home state unknown.** Action: the founder must tell the tax setup which state he is in; the decision tree is (a) register for a seller's permit in that state before the first card sale (physical presence = nexus), (b) check that state's treatment of SaaS/information services for the $50 report, (c) check whether that state taxes separately stated shipping. Stripe Tax can handle rates once registered.
- **Use tax on free demo cards and free replacement cards.** Items bought tax-free on a resale certificate and withdrawn for giveaways or samples are taxable to you at cost (Virginia 23VAC10-210-490: items "withdrawn from inventory for a promotional give-away or other free distribution" are taxed at withdrawal, https://regulations.justia.com/states/virginia/title-23/agency-10/chapter-210/section-23vac10-210-490 ; Georgia Rule 560-12-1-.14; California CDTFA annotations: if sample goods are "specifically purchased for such purpose, the giving of a resale certificate is not proper," https://cdtfa.ca.gov/lawguides/vol2/suta/280-0000-all.html ; https://www.avalara.com/blog/en/north-america/2022/03/what-happens-if-i-buy-tax-exempt-products-then-use-them-for-my-business.html). Practical: pay tax to the supplier on the demo-card order (do not use the resale certificate for it); for the 10 monthly replacement cards, treat them as part of the paid subscription consideration and itemize them at $0 on the invoice, but ask the CPA whether the home state treats them as a taxable withdrawal.
- **Resale certificate:** obtain the seller's permit first, then issue the state's resale certificate to the card supplier for cards bought for resale only.
- **Shipping:** taxable when the item is taxable in roughly half the states (e.g., NY, TX, PA, FL, NJ, MN, NE, LA since 2025); exempt if separately stated and reflecting actual cost in others (e.g., CA, MA, IL); "handling" is taxable almost everywhere, so show shipping and handling as separate lines; Colorado ($0.29) and Minnesota ($0.50) add flat retail delivery fees (https://www.shopify.com/blog/do-you-pay-sales-tax-on-shipping ; https://taxesledger.com/guides/is-shipping-taxable-by-state).
- **200-transaction test:** as of Aug 1, 2026, still used in Arkansas, Georgia, Hawaii, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Nebraska, Nevada, New Jersey, Ohio, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Washington D.C. and Puerto Rico (Connecticut is $100k AND 200; New York is $500k AND 100). Dropped by 17 states including Kentucky (Aug 1, 2026), Illinois, Iowa, Wisconsin (Jan 1, 2026), Utah (Jul 1, 2025), Alaska (Jan 1, 2025) (https://www.avalara.com/blog/en/north-america/2025/06/states-eliminating-economic-nexus-transaction-thresholds.html ; https://www.avalara.com/blog/en/north-america/2026/04/kentucky-removes-transaction-threshold-taxes-data-brokering-services.html ; https://taxcloud.com/blog/sales-tax-nexus-by-state/).

## 14. Outbound-sales compliance for the local test run

- **TCPA (calls/texts):** the statute follows the device, not the audience. Autodialed or prerecorded calls and marketing texts to a cell phone need prior express (written, for marketing) consent even if the number is a business owner's; the B2B carve-out lives only in the Do-Not-Call rules; damages are $500-$1,500 per message (https://www.wipfli.com/insights/articles/does-tcpa-apply-to-b2b-marketing-key-rules ; https://www.fransis.ai/articles/does-tcpa-apply-to-b2b-texts). State mini-TCPAs: Florida FTSA (written consent for automated texts, 8 a.m.-8 p.m., 15-day opt-out safe harbor), Oklahoma OTSA (broader "automated system for the selection or dialing" trigger), Texas SB 140 (eff. Sept 1, 2025: texts covered, private action up to $5,000/violation) (https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2026/07/looking-back-on-the-last-year-of-state-level-tcpa-updates/ ; https://enzodialer.com/resources/compliance/state-mini-tcpa-laws). **Rule for the pilot:** walk in or hand-dial one-to-one from a normal phone; no SMS blasts, no auto-dialer, no texts without a written opt-in.
- **CAN-SPAM (cold email):** the law "makes no exception for business-to-business email"; each message needs accurate headers, a non-deceptive subject, identification as an ad, a valid physical postal address, a working opt-out for at least 30 days, opt-outs honored within 10 business days; penalties "up to $53,088" per email (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business).
- **Texas TDPSA:** applies to any entity doing business in Texas or serving Texas residents that processes or sells personal data and "is not a small business as defined by the SBA"; there is no revenue threshold; the founder's LLC will almost certainly be an SBA small business (most software/services NAICS size standards are tens of millions in receipts), which exempts it from notice/rights duties, but even small businesses may not sell sensitive data without consent; AG enforcement, $7,500/violation, 30-day cure (https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/07/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act-overview ; https://privacylawmap.com/states/texas). Document the SBA size analysis with the NAICS code used. Lead forms plus retargeting pixels would be "targeted advertising" if the exemption ever lapses; keep ad pixels off the site until then.

## 15. Entity, insurance, and a written pilot agreement

- **Form the LLC before** handing out cards or signing pilot restaurants: the vendor can be blamed for a client's profile restriction, the cards are physical goods shipped to third parties, and every agreement (Google consent, Stripe, supplier) should be in the entity's name. Formation cost depends on the unknown home state.
- **Insurance benchmarks** (Vouch, May 21, 2026, medians across 3,000+ startups): general liability $180/yr (range $15-$1,450), tech E&O $3,700/yr ($1,300-$12,400), cyber $2,900/yr ($1,000-$8,800); buy GL when you take space, E&O and cyber at the first customer contract (https://www.vouch.us/blog/startup-insurance-costs). Solo-founder SaaS E&O+cyber bundles are quoted at $2,500-$4,500/yr (https://www.quotesweep.com/blog/tech-startup-insurance-cost). GL excludes professional services, so E&O is the policy that responds to "your advice got my profile suspended."
- **Pilot/demo agreement clauses** (adapted from YC's free sales agreement and Common Paper, https://www.ycombinator.com/sales_agreement, plus beta/trial clause patterns at https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/beta-services/_1): parties and term (e.g., 60 days, either side may end on notice); free cards remain the restaurant's on termination, subscription optional afterward; **written consent** to Manager access and to reading reviews, with a separate checkbox for review replies (satisfies Google's third-party policy); data handling (Takeout/API, reviewer names stripped, raw data purged within 30 days, LLM provider named); "as-is," no guarantee of review count, rating, publication or ranking; Google may filter or restrict; restaurant compliance covenant (no incentives, gating, on-premises pressure, quotas, staff-name requests, no CRFA-void contract clauses, no staff/family reviews); staff-notice obligation; testimonial consent with the material-connection disclosure; liability cap (fees paid or a nominal sum); 7-business-day disassociation promise; governing law.

## 17. Staff names in reviews: what the April 2026 bullet means for the product

Verbatim from https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114: merchants "should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included. This includes: Merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews; Merchants requesting that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member." The FTC's Dec 2025 warning-letter template separately lists "providing compensation to your employees in exchange for the employee obtaining 5-star reviews from friends and family" as a Rule violation (https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2025-Fake-Review-Warning-Template.pdf).

Implications: (1) the "which servers are praised" section may only summarize **organic** mentions; (2) restaurants must not tie bonuses, contests, shift preference or tips to review counts or to being named (a count-based bonus is a quota by another name; a 5-star-conditioned bonus is a §465.4 incentive); (3) no per-server cards, codes, or leaderboards; (4) add to the DON'T list: "Don't pay, bonus, or rank staff on reviews, and don't ask guests to mention anyone by name."

Do not use stock-site "Review us on Google" vectors (unofficial).

## 19. Insider reviews by the founder, friends, and staff during the test run

- **§465.5** applies to an officer or manager reviewing "the business" they work for and to soliciting relatives/employees/agents without disclosure (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/465.5). The founder is not the restaurant's officer, but under the Endorsement Guides a vendor's review of its client is a material connection needing clear disclosure (§255.5), and under Google's policy any review "based on a conflict of interest," including "a contractual or consultory relationship," is removable and can trigger profile restrictions (https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114). Reviews by anyone who did not actually dine are fake reviews under §465.2.
- **Rule:** the founder, the founder's employees, contractors and family do not review pilot restaurants on Google at all; friends who genuinely dine may review on their own, but nobody is asked to as part of the pilot, and coordinated batches would also trip the "unusual volumes or patterns" filter. The pilot agreement should state this so restaurants do not ask either.
